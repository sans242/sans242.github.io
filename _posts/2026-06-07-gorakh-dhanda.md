It started casually — a lazy afternoon, Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan playing in the background. Then a phrase caught my ear: *Tum ek Gorakh Dhandha Ho*. Something about those two words made me stop and rewind.

My first instinct? Classic NFAK mischief — maybe addressing some charming, elusive woman. After all, some of his songs carry that streak (think *Saadgi to hamari zara dekhiye*). But I was completely off.

**"You are an intricate, baffling puzzle"** — and the puzzle was God all along.

The phrase *Gorakh Dhanda* means something complicated, confusing, a riddle that refuses to yield. The word *Gorakh* stood out — it felt distinctly Hindi, almost Braj, rather than Urdu. That oddness pulled me toward its etymology.

**Gorakh**
Guru Gorakhnath — the 11th-century yogi who founded the Nath monastic movement. Known for mystical paradoxes, complex spiritual teachings, and a gift for upending ordinary logic.

**Dhanda**
Business, dealings, trade — the everyday word for what you do, your work, your way of being in the world.

**Together →**
"Gorakhnath's business" — the mysterious, labyrinthine ways of the mystic. A puzzle so deep it defeats ordinary comprehension.

But what truly struck me was the song's real subject. This qawwali is not about a woman. It is addressed to God. The phrase becomes a philosophical declaration — that the Divine is a beautifully baffling mystery, one that hides in plain sight yet defies every attempt at easy understanding.

The genius is in the layering. A 15th-century mystic's reputation, folded into an Urdu word, carried forward through centuries of oral tradition, then placed into a qawwali that sounds like longing but breathes like theology. NFAK didn't just sing it — he made you feel the impossibility of grasping something infinite.

---

**P.S.**
After a bit more researching, in modern usage, *Gorakh Dhanda* has also come to mean shady or questionable dealings — corruption, unethical arrangements, things deliberately made murky. The phrase has traveled from mysticism to political critique. Haryana, in fact, has banned it from official communications entirely. A phrase born to describe the ineffable complexity of God, now used to describe the ineffable complexity of governance. Somehow, that feels fitting.
